\section{Parkinson's disease as a network disorder}

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with motor and non-motor manifestations. Although degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra is a defining pathological feature, the diversity of symptoms cannot be explained by a single brain region. PD affects interactions among distributed cortical, subcortical, motor, cognitive, and limbic systems and is therefore increasingly understood as a network disorder~\cite{Li-Chuan2019Graphtheory,Akbari2023Graphtheory}. The absence of a well-established non-invasive biomarker for early diagnosis or progression monitoring continues to limit the development and evaluation of disease-modifying interventions~\cite{PPMI2011}.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) measures spontaneous blood-oxygen-level-dependent fluctuations in the absence of an explicit task, making it comparatively easy to acquire, standardize, and share across studies. Network neuroscience represents brain regions as nodes and their functional connections as edges, transforming high-dimensional connectivity matrices into interpretable measures of regional and whole-network organization~\cite{Bullmore2009Complex,Wang2010Graph}. Together, rs-fMRI and graph analysis provide a non-invasive framework for investigating distributed functional alterations in PD.

Studies using this framework have reported PD-related alterations in cortico-striatal, sensorimotor, default-mode, and other intrinsic networks~\cite{Jinping2017Graphtheory,Rutvi2021Graphtheory,Akbari2023Graphtheory}. However, findings are not always consistent, and graph estimates depend on preprocessing, connectivity estimation, parcellation, thresholding, software, and computational conditions.

\section{Motivation for reproducibility analysis}

Reproducibility is essential to reliable scientific knowledge and is particularly important in neuroimaging, where findings may ultimately inform clinical research. Open data, shared code, and detailed documentation improve transparency, but do not ensure that complex processing and statistical workflows will produce consistent results.

One challenge is \emph{analytical variability}, which arises from the many defensible choices available in an fMRI workflow, including participant selection, preprocessing, software, parameter settings, and statistical modelling~\cite{carp2012plurality,li2024moving,germani2025hcp}. The Neuroimaging Analysis Replication and Prediction Study demonstrated its practical consequences: 70 teams analyzed the same fMRI dataset using different pipelines and sometimes reached different conclusions~\cite{botvinik2020variability}. Comparisons across neuroimaging tools have likewise shown that software packages, versions, and operating systems can alter derived results~\cite{Bhagwat2021,Glatard2015}.

Graph analyses introduce further flexibility through parcellation, connectivity estimation, preprocessing, and threshold selection. These choices can alter network architecture and contribute to the poor-to-moderate reliability reported for some rs-fMRI graph measures~\cite{Wang2010Graph,Fornito2010Scaling,Telesford2013Reproducibility}.

A less visible challenge is \emph{numerical variability}, which arises from finite-precision computation and differences in hardware, operating systems, mathematical libraries, or parallel execution. Small perturbations can propagate through neuroimaging pipelines and produce meaningful differences in derived outcomes~\cite{Glatard2015,Salari2021,Kiar2020,chatelain2024numerical}. Numerical variability may therefore contribute to the broader analytical variability observed across tools and computational infrastructures~\cite{kiar2024experimental}.

\section{Research problem}

Although numerical variability has been investigated in several structural neuroimaging workflows, fMRI pipelines and their derived graph measures remain comparatively underexplored. Without systematic stability assessment, it is unclear how much computational perturbations contribute to observed graph-measure variation or affect downstream statistical conclusions.

This thesis addresses this gap by asking how numerical variability in fMRIPrep-derived functional-connectivity graph measures compares with between-participant variability, how it propagates to statistical inference, and whether numerical sensitivity can be estimated from a standard functional connectome. Phase~I quantified numerical variability across graph measures, brain regions, network thresholds, populations, and confound-regression strategies, demonstrating that it is measurable and may affect conclusions near a significance threshold. The next phase investigates metric-specific prediction and the complementary possibility of a participant-level indicator of numerical sensitivity.

Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduces numerical variability, Chapter~\ref{chap:phase-one} presents the completed Phase~I study, and Chapter~\ref{chap:future-work} describes the preliminary prediction analysis and proposed future work.
