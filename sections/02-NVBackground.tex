\label{chap:background}

\section{Numerical variability}

Neuroimaging analyses rely on long computational pipelines whose results may be affected by finite-precision arithmetic. Under the IEEE~754 standard, floating-point numbers are represented using a finite number of binary digits. Values that cannot be represented exactly are rounded to a representable value according to the selected rounding mode. Thus, although floating-point formats accommodate a wide range of magnitudes, it is not without its limitations in floating-point arithmetic. Small rounding errors can accumulate or be amplified through operation order, compiler optimizations, hardware, mathematical libraries, operating systems, and parallel execution. This sensitivity is particularly relevant to nonlinear or iterative operations commonly used in registration, segmentation, interpolation, and brain-network modelling~\cite{Glatard2015,Kiar2020,Salari2021}.

In this thesis, \emph{numerical variability} refers to variation in computational outputs caused by such numerical perturbations while the data and analytical method remain fixed. It is the observable consequence of numerical instability: a stable computation produces similar outputs under small perturbations, whereas an unstable computation may amplify them into scientifically relevant differences. Numerical variability can therefore affect computational reproducibility even when code, parameters, and input data are unchanged.

Monte Carlo Arithmetic (MCA) evaluates numerical variability by introducing controlled random perturbations into floating-point operations~\cite{Parker1997MCA,Salari2021}. For a floating-point value $x$, MCA defines an inexact representation as

\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{inexact}(x,t)=x+2^{e_x-t}\xi,
  \label{eq:mca-general}
\end{equation}

where $e_x$ is the exponent of $x$, $t$ is the virtual precision, and $\xi$ is uniformly distributed on $(-1/2,1/2)$. Repeating an instrumented analysis produces a distribution of plausible outputs whose dispersion characterizes the effect of numerical perturbations~\cite{Denis2016Verificarlo,Kiar2021}.

Numerical variability may be summarized by the standard deviation across repeated MCA executions or by the number of significant bits that remain consistent across them. The latter can be estimated as $s_{\mathrm{MCA}}=-\log_2|\hat{\sigma}/\hat{\mu}|$, where $\hat{\mu}$ and $\hat{\sigma}$ are the empirical mean and standard deviation~\cite{Sohier2021Confidence}. A significant bit is a binary digit expected to remain reliable across perturbed executions; larger values indicate greater numerical stability. Outcome-specific measures can also compare numerical variability with other relevant sources of variation. The Numerical--Population Variability Ratio used in this thesis is introduced and defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:phase-one}.

\section{Numerical variability in neuroimaging: literature review}

Initial neuroimaging evidence came from comparisons of computational environments. Analyses performed with different software builds or operating systems produced differences in tissue classification, cortical-thickness estimates, functional components, and motion-correction outputs~\cite{Glatard2015}. Controlled perturbations of mathematical-library calls subsequently reproduced variability associated with operating-system updates while preserving between-participant differences~\cite{Salari2021}. Together, these studies showed that infrastructure-level numerical differences can propagate through complete neuroimaging workflows and affect scientifically relevant outputs.

Numerical perturbation methods have also been evaluated in diffusion-MRI connectome pipelines. A comparison of targeted noise, MCA, random rounding, and operating-system changes found distinct patterns of variation. MCA and random rounding introduced substantial variability, sometimes comparable to differences between participants, and their effects varied markedly across participants~\cite{Kiar2020}. A later study applied sparse and dense MCA to deterministic and probabilistic tractography pipelines. Stability ranged from nearly
complete agreement to only zero or one significant digit. Sparse perturbations sometimes equalled or exceeded session- and participant-level differences, while stable group distributions of graph features concealed unreliable individual estimates~\cite{Kiar2021}. The resulting MCA-generated connectomes were then used to augment an age-classification dataset. Across 2,560 model configurations, several resampling strategies increased mean test-set F1 score from 0.773 to 0.812 and improved agreement between validation and test performance, with little dependence on the number of MCA repetitions for most strategies~\cite{Kiar2021Augmentation}.

Other work has incorporated numerical variability into software validation and evaluated its dependence on the algorithmic context. Numerically informed tests for fMRIPrep distinguished expected variation from meaningful processing changes~\cite{chatelain2024numerical}. Studies of neuroimaging software based on convolutional neural networks found comparatively stable inference but substantial variability during iterative training, with numerical and random-seed perturbations exhibiting related spatial patterns~\cite{Gonzalez2023Inference,Gonzalez2025CNN}. Comparisons of linear-registration tools further demonstrated that stability depends on the software and configuration and suggested that numerical-variability measures may help identify problematic registrations~\cite{mirhakimi2025numerical}.

Recent work has related numerical variability directly to population variability and statistical inference. In structural MRI studies of Parkinson's disease, numerical variability represented a meaningful fraction of between-participant variability and could alter conclusions concerning group differences or clinical associations~\cite{Yohan2025NPVR}. Overall, existing evidence shows that numerical variability depends on the pipeline, participant, outcome, and computational configuration, and that group-level robustness does not guarantee regional or participant-level stability. However, most work has focused on structural or diffusion MRI, registration, segmentation, or structural connectivity. Numerical variability in functional-connectivity graph measures remains comparatively underexplored, motivating the Phase~I study in Chapter~\ref{chap:phase-one}.
