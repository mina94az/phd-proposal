\chapter{Predicting Numerical Variability from Standard Functional Connectivity}
\label{chap:future-work}

\section*{Abstract}

Numerical perturbations introduced during fMRI preprocessing can propagate to functional connectivity (FC) and derived graph measures, potentially contributing to false-positive or false-negative findings near decision thresholds. Nevertheless, numerical variability (NV) is rarely assessed in routine studies because Monte Carlo Arithmetic (MCA) requires repeated processing and specialized instrumentation and remains unfamiliar to many researchers.

This phase investigates whether NV can be predicted from the FC matrix produced by a single standard IEEE execution. The current feasibility analysis focuses on 146 participants with Parkinson's disease and uses retained MCA repetitions only to define participant-level NV targets. Two complementary initial models are considered: M0, a BrainNetCNN model that learns directly from the complete signed FC matrix, and Ridge regression using a compact vector of nine IEEE-derived features. These features summarize threshold proximity, graph density, and the structural consequences of potential edge changes. Across the six reported graph measures and three thresholds, the Ridge model produced higher held-out $R^2$ than M0 for 17 of 18 endpoints and higher Spearman correlation for 16. 

\section{Introduction}

Phase~I demonstrated that NV in fMRI graph measures is measurable and can influence statistical conclusions. Although repeated MCA executions provide an empirical estimate of this uncertainty, their computational cost and specialized instrumentation limit routine use. The main research question of the present phase is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can the numerical variability of graph measures be predicted from the functional connectome produced by a single standard IEEE execution?}
\end{quote}

This question is plausible because MCA perturbations do not affect FC uniformly. Connections close to a graph threshold are particularly susceptible to changing their inclusion status, and the consequence depends on the network location of the affected connection. Thus, a standard IEEE connectome may contain information about numerical sensitivity through its complete FC organization, threshold proximity, and the structural consequences of potential edge changes.

The present work evaluates two initial representations of this information. M0 asks whether a connectome-oriented neural network can learn NV directly from the complete FC matrix. Ridge regression asks whether a compact nine-feature representation is sufficient for prediction. These models serve as initial baselines for investigating the predictability of numerical variability. Further work will explore alternative features, model architectures, and prediction strategies to improve performance, robustness, and interpretability.

\section{Data and methodology}

\subsection{Data, quality control, and prediction targets}

The analysis reused the PPMI data and processing outputs described in Chapter~\ref{chap:phase-one} and was restricted to the Parkinson's disease population. A deliberately minimal quality-control protocol excluded only clear acquisition or processing failures. Each resting-state acquisition was expected to contain 240 volumes; two participants with severely incomplete series of 92 and 13 volumes were excluded. Participants were also excluded when spatial registration failed or visual inspection revealed gross image-integrity defects, including large signal voids or pronounced spatial discontinuities. Within retained acquisitions, volumes flagged as non-steady-state were excluded before FC estimation.
After quality control and matching to the current prediction cohort, 146 participants had a valid IEEE connectome and retained MCA outputs. Of these, 140 had ten retained MCA repetitions, five had nine, and one had eight.

As an initial feasibility experiment, the models were evaluated at three of the six absolute-correlation thresholds examined in Chapter~\ref{chap:phase-one}: 0.05, 0.20, and 0.50. We report the same six binary graph measures: degree centrality, betweenness centrality, clustering coefficient, and eigenvector centrality as local measures, and average shortest path length and small-worldness as global measures. The prediction target was the MCA-derived numerical variability of each graph measure. For a local measure, its standard deviation across retained MCA repetitions was calculated separately at each of the 100 regions and then averaged across regions. For a global measure, the target was its standard deviation across repetitions. All reported targets were available for 146 participants except average shortest path length and small-worldness at threshold 0.50, for which 116 participants had finite targets. The preliminary models therefore attempt to predict participant-level graph-measure variability from a single IEEE connectome, without requiring MCA repetitions at prediction time.

\subsection{Initial prediction models}

\paragraph{M0: Full-connectome baseline.}
M0 uses the complete signed IEEE FC matrix (100 × 100), without thresholding or engineered features. A BrainNetCNN encoder processes the connectivity matrix through edge-to-edge and edge-to-node operations [34], followed by a fully connected layer and measure-specific outputs. The model jointly predicts numerical variability for six graph measures using a shared representation of the FC matrix. Model configurations were selected through inner validation, and predictions from two independently trained models were averaged for each outer fold. M0 serves as an initial benchmark for comparison with feature-based prediction models.

\paragraph{Auxiliary features.}
To investigate whether numerical sensitivity can be explained by more specific properties of the IEEE connectome, we construct nine features for each graph measure and threshold. These features describe three aspects of the thresholded network: threshold proximity, graph density, and structural influence.

Threshold proximity describes how close FC values are to the threshold. Edges near the threshold are more likely to change their inclusion status under small numerical perturbations. However, not all edge changes have the same consequences: adding or removing an edge may substantially affect a graph
measure in one location while having little effect elsewhere.

We therefore introduce structural influence features to estimate the potential consequences of such changes. Rather than perturbing FC values, we hypothetically toggle individual edges in the IEEE-derived graph (adding an absent edge or removing a present edge) and measure the resulting change in the graph statistic. This provides a measure-specific estimate of how sensitive the network is to changes in particular connections.

For a threshold $\tau$, let $C_{s,e}$ denote the IEEE FC value of edge $e$ for participant $s$. Its distance from the threshold is defined as
\begin{equation}
    d_{s,e}^{(\tau)}
    = \left|\,|C_{s,e}|-\tau\,\right|.
\end{equation}

Among the $M=4{,}950$ possible undirected edges, we consider those satisfying $d_{s,e}^{(\tau)}\leq 0.02$ and evaluate at most
the 200 closest edges.

For a local graph measure $g$, let $\delta_{s,e,i}^{(g,\tau)}$ denote the change in the measure at region $i$ after toggling edge $e$. We summarize the effect of that edge across the $N=100$ regions using its mean absolute and root-mean-square changes:
\begin{equation}
    a_{s,e}^{(g,\tau)}
    = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
      \left|\delta_{s,e,i}^{(g,\tau)}\right|,
    \qquad
    r_{s,e}^{(g,\tau)}
    = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
      \left(\delta_{s,e,i}^{(g,\tau)}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}

For a global graph measure, both quantities reduce to the
absolute change in that measure:
\begin{equation}
    a_{s,e}^{(g,\tau)}
    = r_{s,e}^{(g,\tau)}
    = \left|\delta_{s,e}^{(g,\tau)}\right|.
\end{equation}

Only candidate edges producing finite responses are retained when aggregating these effects into the structural influence
features. The resulting nine features, including four threshold-proximity features, graph density, and four structural
influence features, are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:compact-ridge-features}.

\paragraph{M1: Compact Ridge regression.}
M1 uses the nine IEEE-derived features described above to predict NV separately for each graph measure and threshold.
M1 explicitly represents two hypothesized determinants of numerical sensitivity: the presence of edges near the threshold and the potential impact of changing those edges on graph measures. Graph density additionally characterizes the thresholded network.

A separate Ridge regression model is fitted for each graph measure and threshold. Missing values are handled using training-only preprocessing, and numeric features are standardized before fitting. The regularization strength is selected through inner validation, and predictions are
transformed back to the original NV scale.

M1 therefore provides a compact, interpretable comparison with M0 and allows us to assess whether these mechanism-informed
features are sufficient to explain and predict numerical variability.

\subsection{Subject-level evaluation}

M0 and Ridge were evaluated using identical participants, targets, and ten subject-level outer folds, with all preprocessing and model selection restricted to the corresponding training data. Pooled held-out predictions were assessed using $R^2$ and Spearman rank correlation. M0 is reported as the mean and standard deviation across three initialization settings, whereas Ridge is deterministic for fixed folds. Independent validation remains necessary to assess generalizability.
\section{Preliminary results}

Table~\ref{tab:m0-ridge-results} summarizes the 18 measure--threshold endpoints. Ridge achieved higher held-out $R^2$ than M0 for 17 endpoints and higher Spearman correlation for 16. Improvements occurred for all outcomes at threshold 0.05 and all except average shortest path length at threshold 0.20. At threshold 0.50, Ridge achieved higher $R^2$ for all six outcomes, whereas M0 had a slightly higher Spearman correlation for small-worldness.

\section{Discussion and future work}

The results suggest that numerical sensitivity is partly encoded in a participant's standard connectome. M0 provides a full-connectome benchmark, whereas Ridge uses mechanism-informed summaries of threshold proximity, density, and structural influence. Metric-specific prediction preserves differences among graph measures and helps identify their sources of sensitivity. A complementary participant-level indicator, such as the mean or maximum standardized NV or a data-driven component score, could simplify interpretation and screening but would discard metric-specific information.

The usefulness of a global indicator may depend on threshold: the first principal component explained approximately 95\%, 89\%, and 59\% of standardized NV variance across measures at thresholds 0.05, 0.20, and 0.50, respectively. Future work will compare metric-specific and global formulations. One possible extension is a hybrid model that combines a learned IEEE-FC representation with the nine auxiliary features through a multilayer perceptron. Any resulting model will require stability assessment and validation on independent datasets.
