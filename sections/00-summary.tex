\chapter*{Summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Summary}
\begin{onehalfspace}


Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) and graph theory offer a non-invasive way to study network-level alterations associated with Parkinson's disease (PD). Their scientific and eventual clinical value depends on results that remain reliable when analytical choices or computational environments change within reasonable bounds. As analyses become more computationally complex, ensuring their numerical reliability becomes more challenging. Small perturbations during processing can propagate through complex pipelines, leading to variability in outcomes and raising concerns about reproducibility. Systematic evaluation of pipeline stability can help determine whether results remain within acceptable numerical limits.

This PhD proposal investigates the numerical variability of functional MRI graph measures. Its central objective is to systematically evaluate variability in graph measures of functional connectivity and translate the findings into practical guidance for robust studies. Two projects define the scope of this work.

Project~I quantified numerical variability in graph measures derived from Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) data processed with fMRIPrep. The project is complete, and the manuscript has been accepted for publication in \textit{PLOS ONE}. Ten Monte Carlo Arithmetic (MCA) realizations were generated per participant. The Numerical--Population Variability Ratio (\npvratio{}) expressed numerical variability relative to between-participant variability. Across the local and global graph measures, mean \npvratio{} values ranged from approximately 0.02 to 0.19 and depended on the graph metric, brain region, network threshold, population, and confound-regression strategy. For nominal $p$-values from 0.01 to 0.09, the estimated mean probability of a numerically induced significance flip was 17.7\%. These results show that computational noise is measurable and can affect statistical conclusions near a significance threshold.


Project II, still ongoing, investigates whether a single standard IEEE functional connectome per participant can predict numerical variability in individual graph measures. Project I showed that numerical variability is an important confound in neuroimaging analyses, but quantifying it currently requires either repeated Monte Carlo Arithmetic (MCA) runs, which carry a heavy computational burden, or specialised hardware. A predictive model could serve as the basis for a practical tool to estimate numerical variability without that burden. Preliminary results suggest that threshold proximity and the structural consequences of potential edge changes provide predictive information. The proposed work will test ways to improve prediction, assess generalizability across subgroups, and determine when a single participant-level score can identify numerically sensitive participants. It will also examine whether predicted variability can support estimates of \npvratio{} and significance-flip risk without repeated MCA processing.

\end{onehalfspace}
